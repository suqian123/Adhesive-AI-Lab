A B S T R A C T
Self–healing polyurethanes relying on a single dynamic covalent bond often face trade-offs between mechanical 
performance and healing efficiency. In this work, we developed an innovative self-healing elastomer by stra­
tegically incorporating imine bonds into a Diels–Alder (DA) adduct-based polyurethane. The resulting material 
exhibited exceptional mechanical properties, achieving a tensile strength of 26.9 MPa and an elongation at break 
of 513.3%. Remarkably, PUDI retained 90.0% of its tensile strength and recovered 71.9% of dissipated energy 
after 10 fatigue cycles at room temperature. This dual dynamic bonds system enabled outstanding self–healing 
performance, complete healing at 120 ◦C—significantly surpassing conventional DA–based polyurethanes. 
Moreover, the material maintained a high repair capability at reduced temperatures, demonstrating consistent 
healing efficiencies of 94.3%, 90.9%, and 79.2% over three damage–repair cycles. Notably, the incorporation of 
dynamic imine bonds significantly enhanced degradation performance, primarily due to improved chain mobility 
and dynamic network rearrangement. This work overcomes fundamental limitations of traditional self-healing 
polyurethanes, offering a promising platform for advanced applications in flexible electronics, protective coat­
ings, and biomedical devices.
1. Introduction
Self-healing polymers have garnered significant research interest due 
to their unique ability to autonomously repair damage, thereby 
extending material lifespan and reducing maintenance costs [1–4]. 
These materials primarily employ two healing mechanisms: (1) dynamic 
covalent bonds [5,6], including Diels-Alder adducts (DA adducts) [7–9], 
imine bonds [10–13], disulfide bonds [14–16], acylhydrazone bonds 
[17–19], and diselenide bonds [20–22]. (2) non-covalent interactions 
such as hydrogen bonding [23,24], metal-ligand coordination [25,26], 
and host-guest complexation [27,28].
Polyurethane, a highly versatile polymer, has been extensively 
engineered with dynamic covalent bonds to achieve self-healing prop­
erties [29–32]. However, relying on a single type of dynamic bond may 
limit the repair efficiency and environmental adaptability of poly­
urethanes. For example, DA adduct-based polyurethanes generally 
require high healing temperatures (≥120 ◦C), exhibit lower self–healing 
efficiency, and need prolonged healing durations [33], while imine 
bond-based systems often suffer from reduced mechanical stability due 
to excessive chain mobility. To address these challenges, many self­
–healing polyurethanes have been developed using combined dynamic 
bonds, including both dynamic covalent and dynamic noncovalent 
bonds. Zhu et al. [34] engineered a dual-bond system combining DA 
adducts and disulfide bonds, achieving a tensile strength of 9.87 MPa 
with 90.1% healing efficiency. Mantala and Crespy [35] reported a se­
ries of poly(urea–urethane) transparent elastomers containing revers­
ible disulfide and hydrogen bonds, and revealed outstanding ability to 
heal at room temperature with a healing efficiency as high as 95%. Ye 
et al. [36] introduced disulfide bonds into an imine exchange reac­
tion–based polyurethane, developing a polyurethane with enhanced 
repair capabilities, including a tensile strength of 12.95 MPa, a first 
repair efficiency of 92.5%, and a third repair efficiency of 73.4%.

ties of polyurethane by incorporating multiple dynamic bonds, enabling

2.2. Synthesis of chain extender containing imine bonds (VP)
The synthetic mechanism of the imine-containing chain extender 
(VP) is illustrated in Fig. 1. In a typical procedure, p-phenylenediamine 
was first dissolved in tetrahydrofuran in a three-necked flask equipped 
with a stirrer and condenser, maintained at 45 ◦C in an oil bath. Vanillin, 
in a 2:1 M ratio to p–phenylenediamine, dissolved in tetrahydrofuran, 
was then added dropwise via a constant-pressure funnel. After 4 h of 
reaction, the mixture was concentrated by rotary evaporation and 
precipitated in ethanol (twice the volume). The resulting solid was 
collected by filtration, purified through two recrystallizations from 
toluene/hexane (10:1 v/v), and dried. The obtained VP is a light yellow 
crystal with a yield of 93%.
The successful synthesis of VP was confirmed through comprehen­
sive characterization using 1H–NMR and FT–IR spectroscopy (see in 
Supporting Information, Figs. S1 and S2).

bonds(PUDI)

is illustrated in Fig. 2, with detailed reagent quantities provided in

h to achieve furan end–capping.

2.4. Characterization
Chemical structure. The chemical structural characterization of the 
synthesized compounds was conducted using spectroscopic techniques. 
Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FT–IR, VERTEX 70, Germany) 
was employed to analyze the chemical structures of VP, PU–DA, and 
PUDI over the spectral range of 500–4000 cm−1. Proton nuclear mag­
netic resonance spectroscopy (1H NMR) was performed using a Bruker 
AVANCE III HD 500 MHz NMR spectrometer (Germany).
Thermal properties and thermal reversibility performance. The 
thermal properties of PU-DA and PUDI were investigated using differ­
ential scanning calorimetry (DSC, TA Q2000, USA). Samples were 
analyzed from −80 ◦C to 200 ◦C at a heating rate of 10 ◦C/min under a 
nitrogen atmosphere. Additionally, the thermal reversibility was eval­
uated through sol–gel transition tests conducted at 130 ◦C and 60 ◦C, 
respectively.
Mechanical properties. The mechanical properties were evaluated 
through tensile and cyclic tensile tests conducted on a Shimadzu AG–10 
kN universal testing machine (Japan) at a constant speed of 50 mm/min.
Self–healing performance. To evaluate the self-healing performance, 
standardized scratch damage (0.5 mm deep) was introduced on the 
specimen surfaces. The damaged samples were then subjected to ther­
mal repair at varying temperatures and durations. The evolution of 
scratch morphology during heat treatment was observed using polarized 
optical microscopy (Axioskop 2 plus, Zeiss, Germany). For quantitative 
evaluation, the specimen was completely cut into two pieces, and the 
fractured ends were brought into contact before thermal treatment. The 
recovery of tensile strength was measured after heat treatment. Healing 
efficiencies (η) for single and multiple repair cycles were calculated 
using Equation (1), enabling a quantitative comparison of the self-

3. Results and discussion
3.1. Chemical structure of polyurethane with double dynamic bonds 
(PUDI)
The PUDI elastomer was successfully synthesized by incorporating 
both DA adducts and imine bonds, as confirmed by FT–IR analysis 
(Fig. 3a). Key spectral features included: (1) the characteristic DA 
adduct absorption at 1775 cm−1; (2) imine bond (C––N) stretching at 
1654 cm−1; and (3) a shifted carbonyl (C––O) vibration at 1709 cm−1 
compared to PU-DA (1654 cm−1). Additional peaks at 3310 cm−1 
(H–bonded N–H), 2968/2865 cm−1 (–CH2/–CH3), and the absence of 
–NCO signals (2200–2280 cm−1) further verified complete reaction and 
structural integrity. These collective spectroscopic evidences unambig­
uously demonstrated the successful formation of PUDI with both dy­
namic covalent networks.
3.2. Thermal property and thermal reversibility of PUDI
The thermal behavior of PU–DA and PUDI was characterized by DSC 
(Fig. 3b). Both materials exhibited distinct glass transitions tempera­
tures (Tg) at −36.2 ◦C for PU–DA and −31.8 ◦C for PUDI. The 4.4 ◦C 
increase in Tg for PUDI indicated enhanced chain rigidity due to the 
incorporation of imine bonds. This structural modification improved 
polymer chain regularity and network connectivity, thereby requiring 
greater thermal energy for segmental motion. Additionally, an endo­
thermic peak observed at 130 ◦C corresponded to the retro–Diels–Alder 
(r–DA) reaction of the DA adducts, confirming their thermal reversibility 
within the polyurethane matrix.
The thermal reversibility of PU–DA and PUDI was evaluated using a 
sol–gel transition test at 130 ◦C and 60 ◦C (Fig. 3c and d). When heated 
at 130 ◦C for 10 min, both elastomers–initially cut into pieces with 
minimal DMF)–transformed from solids into viscous liquids (Fig. 3c1, 
d1), demonstrating DA bond cleavage. The average molecular weight 
and polydispersity index (PDI, Mw/Mn) of the synthesized polyurethanes 
were measured by gel permeation chromatography (GPC), as shown in 
Table S3 in the supporting information. It was evident that the synthe­
sized polyurethanes exhibited high molecular weights, however, the 
number-average molecular weight (Mn) decreased significantly. Taking 
PUD1.5I0.5 as an example: the original Mn was 32560 g/mol, which 
decreased to approximately 14263 g/mol after treatment at 130 ◦C for 
10 min. Subsequent heating at 60 ◦C for 120 min enabled DA recom­
bination, restoring the solid state (Fig. 3c2,d2). This reversible transition 
remained consistent through three complete cycles (Fig. 3c1–c6,d1–d6), 
confirming the excellent thermal responsiveness of both materials.
These results demonstrated that both PU–DA and PUDI exhibited 
thermally reversible solid-fluid transitions through alternating heating 
at 130 ◦C and 60 ◦C. The phase transformation mechanism involved 
three key processes: (1) At 130 ◦C, r–DA reaction (indicated by the DSC

3.3.1. Tensile properties
This study systematically evaluated the mechanical properties of 
PUDI using tensile testing (Fig. 4). The stress-strain curves (Fig. 4a) 
demonstrated that increasing the imine bond content in PU-DA2.0 
resulted in a clear trade–off: while elongation at break was enhanced (up 
to 712.6% for PUD0.5I1.5), tensile strength reduced (from 27.6 MPa to 
6.4 MPa). Optimal mechanical performance was achieved with a DA: 
imine ratio of 1.5:0.5 (PUD1.5I0.5), exhibiting balanced properties (26.9 
MPa strength, 513.3% elongation). Correspondingly, material toughness 
decreased progressively with higher imine content, from 39.5 MJ/m3 
(PUD1.5I0.5) to 17.3 MJ/m3 (PUD0.5I1.5), as illustrated in Fig. 4b.
The mechanical property variations in PUDI result from competing 
effects of its dual dynamic networks: (1) thermally stable DA adducts, 
which maintain structural integrity at ambient temperature and 
providing strength–toughness through permanent crosslinking; and (2) 
room-temperature dynamic imine bonds, which reduce mechanical 
stability through continuous exchange reactions but enhance chain 
flexibility. As the imine bond content increases, this trade–off becomes 
more pronounced—the resulting higher chain mobility improves elon­
gation at break (up to 712%) but progressively diminishes tensile 
strength and toughness due to network instability.
3.3.2. Fatigue resistance
To evaluate fatigue resistance under practical loading conditions, 
PU–DA and PUDI were subjected to repeated cyclic tensile tests at a 
constant strain of 120%. The hysteresis loop area in the stress-strain 
curves (Fig. 5) was quantified as dissipated energy [32,33], providing 
a direct measure of the materials' energy dissipation capacity and 
structural integrity during prolonged mechanical stress.
The cyclic tensile behavior of PU–DA2.0 and PUD1.5I0.5 was compared 
in Fig. 5a and b, with a quantitative analysis presented in Fig. 5c. The 
results revealed that the dissipated energy of PUD1.5I0.5 was lower than 
that of PU–DA2.0. This reduction indicated that the incorporation of 
imine bonds decreased energy storage capacity through two mecha­
nisms: (1) a reduction in hydrogen bonding density and (2) enhanced 
chain segment regularity. These structural modifications promoted 
greater molecular mobility, enabling more efficient energy dissipation 
during cyclic loading while maintaining the material's fatigue resistance 
[36,41].
A greater amount of energy was required to align the molecular 
chains during the first tensile cycle, resulting in a larger hysteresis loop 
area and enhanced energy dissipation. The stress relaxation behavior of 
both PU–DA and PUDI elastomers was characterized by decreasing 
hysteresis loop areas and dissipated energy with cycling (Fig. 5c), which 
arose from intrinsic chain alignment and the disordered arrangement of 
polymer chains. Moreover, timely tensile loading prevented complete 
structural recovery between cycles. After a recovery period of 1 h at 
room temperature, both the tensile strength and dissipated energy 
returned to relatively elevated levels. A comparison of the recovery rates 
of tensile strength and dissipated energy between PU–DA2.0 and 
PUD1.5I0.5 (Fig. 5d) revealed that both elastomers exhibit a degree of 
fatigue resistance, with PUD1.5I0.5 showing better energy dissipation 
recovery (~71.9% vs ~69.3% for PU-DA2.0 after 1h rest) due to the 
incorporation of imine bonds. These dynamic bonds enable (1) 
enhanced chain mobility for segmental rearrangement, and (2) room­
–temperature exchange reactions that partially restore the network 
structure. The combined effects significantly improve fatigue resistance 
compared to DA–only systems.

To quantitatively assess self–healing performance, PU–DA and PUDI 
samples underwent tensile testing following a two–stage thermal treat­
ment (Fig. 6b and c): an initial repair at 120 ◦C to activate the dynamics 
of DA adducts, followed by annealing at 60 ◦C to enable DA adduct 
reformation. This dual–temperature protocol ensures complete network 
reconstruction.
The tensile strength data (Fig. 6b and c) revealed significant differ­
ences in healing efficiency between PU–DA2.0 and PUD1.5I0.5. After 
complete thermal treatment (120 ◦C for 10 min followed by 60 ◦C for 
120 min), PU–DA2.0 recovered 94.0% of its original strength (28.6 MPa 
→ 26.9 MPa), while PUD1.5I0.5 achieved 100% recovery (26.9 MPa → 
27.2 MPa) due to imine bond exchange reactions during the 60 ◦C 
annealing. Notably, even without the low–temperature treatment, 
PUD1.5I0.5 maintained 84.2% efficiency compared to PU–DA2.0's 53.2%. 
Therefore, PUD1.5I0.5 demonstrated superior healing efficiency due to 
the combination of imine bonds and DA adducts, highlighting the dual 
advantages of imine bonds: (1) enabling chain rearrangement at lower 
temperatures through enhanced segmental mobility [33], which is 
beneficial for forming more DA bonds than PU–DA without imine bonds; 
and (2) facilitating PCL chain realignment and improving the regularity 
of PCL segments. These effects further enhance the tensile strength. The 
superior performance results from the dynamic exchange capability of 
imine bonds, which compensates for the temperature-dependent refor­
mation limitations of DA adducts.
The incorporation of imine bonds can significantly enhance the 
self–healing capabilities of PU–DA elastomers. Therefore, the low­
–temperature (90 ◦C) self-healing performance of PUD1.5I0.5 and 
PU–DA2.0 was comparatively evaluated (Fig. 7a and b). PUD1.5I0.5 ach­
ieved complete scratch repair within 10 min, whereas PU–DA2.0 
exhibited incomplete healing even after 120 min. Quantitative analysis 
revealed outstanding healing efficiencies of 94.3%, 90.9%, and 79.2% 
over three repair cycles for PUD1.5I0.5. This remarkable performance 
resulted from the dual functionality of imine bonds: (1) increasing chain 
flexibility to facilitate molecular diffusion to damaged areas, and (2) 
promoting PCL chain realignment through dynamic exchange reactions. 
These synergistic effects enable efficient low–temperature repair that is 
unattainable by DA–adduct–only systems.
As shown in Fig. 7c, the developed PUDI system outperformed 
recently reported self–healing materials [15,16,35,36,42–46] by 
simultaneously achieving both high tensile strength (26.9 MPa) and 
exceptional healing efficiency (100%), a combination rarely attained in 
existing systems. This breakthrough performance stems from the 
synergistic integration of dynamic DA adducts and imine bonds, which 
collectively provide structural stability while enabling efficient network 
reformation.
3.5. Exploration of self–healing mechanism of PUDI
The PUDI elastomer was developed by strategically incorporating 
imine bonds into a DA–based polyurethane network, creating a dual 
dynamic covalent system comprising DA adducts and imine bonds. 
Comprehensive characterization revealed that PUDI achieved an 
optimal balance between mechanical strength (26.9 MPa tensile 
strength) and healing efficiency (100%), attributable to synergistic in­
teractions among (1) thermally reversible DA bonds, (2) dynamic imine 
bonds, (3) hydrogen bonding networks, and (4) flexible PCL segments. 
The repair mechanism is illustrated in Fig. 8.
During service, PUDI elastomers are inevitably subjected to various 
external stresses and environmental factors that can induce macroscopic 
damage, such as crack initiation and propagation. This macroscopic 
damage in polymeric materials fundamentally originating from micro­
structural transformations. Heat treatment can induce alteration at the 
molecular level, and promote self–repair. At elevated temperatures, 
such as 120 ◦C, the elastomer network undergoes three simultaneous 
dynamic processes: (1) retro–Diels–Alder (r-DA) reactions cleave DA 
adducts, (2) imine bonds participate in exchange reactions, and (3) 
hydrogen bonds undergo reversible dissociation. These transformations 
generate mobile polymer fragments while simultaneously enhancing the 
flexibility of PCL segments. The liberated chain segments then migrate 
toward damaged regions via thermally activated diffusion, where they 
initiate preliminary repair by physically filling microcracks. Concur­
rently, the reduction in network constraints allows PCL chains to ach­
ieve enhanced molecular alignment through thermal motion, 
establishing the foundation for complete property restoration.
Subsequent annealing at 60 ◦C promotes comprehensive network 
reconstruction through multiple pathways: DA adducts recombine, 
imine bonds reorganize via dynamic exchange, and hydrogen bonds 
reestablish their connectivity. This triple–rebonding mechanism effec­
tively restores the material's architecture while optimizing the packing 
of PCL chains. Notably, the incorporated imine bonds extend the healing 
capability to lower temperatures (from 120 ◦C to 90 ◦C) by enabling (1) 
enhanced chain mobility, (2) continuous bond exchange, and (3) partial 
activation of DA adducts. The synergistic interplay among these dy­
namic covalent bonds, hydrogen bonding, and chain rearrangement 
endows PUDI with superior healing efficiency over an extended tem­
perature range compared to conventional systems relying on a single 
dynamic bond type.

\n--- PAGE 8 ---\n3.6. Degradation performance of PUDI
As PCL is a semi–crystalline biodegradable polyester, and consid­
ering the environmental pollution caused by waste polyurethane, 
incorporating PCL into polyurethane chains inevitably imparts degrad­
ability to the resulting material. Therefore, the degradation performance 
of the synthesized self-healing polyurethane was investigated. The PUDI 
sample was immersed in a 1 mol/L NaOH solution. The change in the 
samples and their weight were periodically monitored and analyzed, as 
shown in Fig. 9. For comparison, PU–DA was used as a control. It was 
observed that the solution exhibited a visible color change after the 
PUDI sample had been immersed for 30 days, accompanied by a 
noticeable Tyndall effect in the container (Fig. 9b). These results suggest 
that PUDI underwent gradual degradation in the alkaline solution, 
releasing numerous degradation fragments into the surrounding me­
dium. Over time, the solution color transitioned from light yellow to 
brown, and the Tyndall effect became increasingly pronounced. The 
degradation mechanism of PUDI can be explained as follows: the ester 
bonds in the urethane groups and long–chain PCL segments are sus­
ceptible to alkaline hydrolysis. Under strong alkaline conditions, these 
bonds progressively break down [47], leading to fragmentation of mo­
lecular chains and their release into the solution, which accounts for the 
significant weight loss.
In contrast, when PU–DA was immersed in the same alkaline solution 
for 30 days, a Tyndall effect also appeared (Fig. 9a). However, after 90 
days of immersion, the solution color only turned slightly yellow. This 
indicates that PUDI degrades more rapidly than PU–DA. Concurrently, 
sample weights were monitored, and the weight loss data are presented 
in Fig. 9c. The weight loss rate for both samples increased progressively 
throughout the degradation period, reaching 40% for PU–DA and 67% 
for PUDI after 90 days. Consistently, the mass loss curves confirmed that 
PUDI degraded significantly faster than PU–DA. This difference in 
degradation behavior can be attributed to variations in chain flexibility. 
Unlike PU-DA, PUDI incorporates dynamic imine bonds into its polymer 
chains, which impart higher flexibility at room temperature. As a result, 
hydroxide ions can more readily penetrate the polyurethane matrix in 
PUDI and access the ester bonds, making it more susceptible to alkaline 
hydrolysis and chain cleavage. Therefore, PUDI degrades more rapidly 
than PU-DA.
4. Conclusions
This study successfully developed a novel self–healing polyurethane 
(PUDI) by incorporating dual dynamic covalent structures comprising 
Diels–Alder (DA) adducts and imine bonds. The PUDI elastomer 
exhibited outstanding mechanical performance, with a tensile strength 
of 26.9 MPa, elongation at break of 513.3%, and toughness of 39.5 MJ/ 
m3. Remarkably, it maintains 90.0% tensile strength recovery and 
71.9% energy dissipation recovery after 10 fatigue cycles followed by 1 
h of rest at room temperature, demonstrating exceptional fatigue resis­
tance. The material showed superior healing capabilities, achieving 
complete repair at 120 ◦C and maintaining excellent multiple repair 
performance even at reduced temperatures, representing a significant 
improvement over conventional PU–DA systems. Additionally, the 
incorporation of dynamic imine bonds imparted greater flexibility at 
room temperature, resulting in enhanced degradation performance of 
PUDI. These exceptional properties arose from the synergistic combi­
nation of the dynamic exchange capabilities of imine bonds and DA 
adducts, enabling simultaneous high mechanical strength and efficient 
self-repair.
